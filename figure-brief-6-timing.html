⏱ Timing & Communication:
Moving as One

Timing is the foundation of successful stunting. When base and flyer move as one unified rhythm, magic happens — and the magic is trainable.

FIG 6A

The count system — "1 — Up — 2 — pull — Hit"

"1"
Both: SET. Grips confirmed, breath in, everything still — the next four counts never start from chaos.
"UP"
Flyer: steps and loads in.
Base: hands take her weight. From here, both athletes share one clock.
"2"
Both: THE DIP — his quick shallow bend and her loading bend happen as ONE motion. Two separate dips = two separate stunts.
"PULL"
Base: drives up through the legs.
Flyer: jumps and pulls tall at the exact same instant — forces adding, not colliding.
"HIT"
Both: lockout, squeeze, stillness. The count ends at the top of the STILLNESS, not the top of the motion.
The one unbreakable rule: the rhythm never changes. Same words, same speed, every rep, all season — the call IS the pair's shared clock. (Different words at your gym? Fine. The words matter less than the constancy.)
Why timing beats strength: when his drive and her jump peak together, their power ADDS — she feels light. When her jump lands late, she lands ON his power instead — and a strong pair suddenly "feels weak." It was never strength.
FIG 6B

Building rhythm together — three proven drills

🙈 The Blind Count Drill

Both partners close their eyes and sync the dip on the count — ground dips and shoulder-height loads only, spotter present. Closing the eyes forces the pair onto the two channels that actually matter at height: the rhythm in their ears and the pressure in their hands and feet.

You've passed when: ten eyes-closed dips in a row land on the same beat — and both partners can FEEL when one was off.

🎵 The Metronome Drill

Set a metronome to 60–80 BPM and dip on every beat. The metronome is an honesty machine — it never speeds up when you're excited or drags when you're tired, which is exactly what human counting does without noticing.

You've passed when: the coach can't tell rep 1 from rep 10.

🎥 Film & Review

Record your stunts, watch in slow motion TOGETHER, and count out loud over the footage. Find the seam — the exact frame where her jump meets his drive. One continuous motion = clean; a visible hitch = the timing gap you couldn't feel at full speed.

You've passed when: the pair spots its own timing errors before the coach does.
FIG 6C

How partners talk — before, during, after

BEFORE the rep: name the skill, name the exit — "Extension, cradle down." Two seconds that prevent two athletes doing two different stunts. Changes get said BEFORE "1," never invented mid-air.
"DOWN"

Anyone can call any stunt down, any time — honoured instantly, never argued. An athlete afraid to say "I'm off" hides exactly the signal everyone needs.

"HOLD"

Freeze and squeeze — the base is fixing something. The flyer goes statue-mode: stay tight, change nothing.

"CHECK"

Something feels off but isn't failing: re-squeeze, re-stack, finish the rep.

AFTER the rep — the 30-second debrief: What did you feel? What drifted? Who felt it first? Reps teach the body; debriefs teach the partnership.
"The flyer trusts what she feels, not what she sees. Make her feel your rhythm."
How trust actually works: every identical rep is a promise kept — enough of them and her body stops bracing for surprises. What breaks it: surprises, and above all an ignored down-call. How it's repaired: not with speeches — with fast, identical, guaranteed wins lower on the ladder, and the ignored down-call made right out loud first.

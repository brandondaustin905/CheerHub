The system is stable when two conditions hold: the sum of forces equals zero (the base supports the combined weight) and the sum of moments equals zero (no net torque rotates the system). The controlling variable is the horizontal position of the COMBINED centre of mass (COM) relative to the base's base of support (BOS) — the area under and between his feet. Reference values: in anatomical standing, an adult's COM lies at roughly 53–57% of standing height, near the sacral region — slightly higher proportionally in males (greater shoulder/torso mass) and lower in females (greater pelvic/thigh mass distribution). When a flyer raises her arms or lifts a leg into a shape, her COM elevates and shifts toward the lifted mass. This is why body positions are perturbations, not poses: every shape relocates the mass the base must keep stacked. TORQUE: THE ENEMY WITH A LEVER ARM Torque = force × moment arm. A worked example (illustrative): a 55 kg flyer exerts approximately 540 N of weight force. If her COM drifts 2 cm horizontally past the base's hands, the system develops roughly 10.8 N·m of torque the base must counter. At 5 cm, 27 N·m. The relationship is linear — which is why early corrections are cheap and late corrections are expensive, and why the coaching maxim "a small early step beats a big late one" is a physics statement, not a preference. THE LOAD PATH In a well-built extension, force travels: flyer's foot → base's palm → stacked (neutral) wrist → forearm long axis → extended elbow → elevated scapula → trunk → pelvis → femur → tibia → foot → floor. Every joint held in-line transmits force through bone alignment at low muscular cost. Every joint out of line converts body-weight support into a continuous muscular torque problem — the biomechanical definition of a "leaky" stunt. The sequencing is proximal-to-distal in force origin: legs → hips → trunk → arms. The arms steer and finish; the legs launch. WHY THE LEGS: the knee and hip extensors (quadriceps, gluteus maximus, hamstrings as hip extensors) plus the plantarflexors (gastrocnemius, soleus) constitute the largest force-producing musculature in the body, capable of multiples of what the shoulder girdle can press. A base who initiates with his arms has volunteered his weakest engine. THE STRETCH-SHORTENING CYCLE (SSC): the dip is a countermovement. A rapid, shallow eccentric loading of the extensors stores elastic energy in the muscle-tendon units and potentiates the concentric drive via the stretch reflex. The SSC's contribution decays with time — a slow or deep dip dissipates the stored energy as heat. This is the mechanism behind the cue "dip like a jump, not a squat": the dip's job is elastic potentiation, not range of motion. DIP QUALITY MARKERS (mechanical definitions): - DEPTH: quarter-squat range. Beyond it, the amortization phase lengthens and SSC return collapses. - TURNAROUND SPEED: the eccentric-concentric transition should be near-instant; the transition duration, not the depth, predicts drive quality. - TRUNK VERTICALITY: the drive vector points where the trunk points. A forward trunk lean redirects ground reaction force anteriorly — producing flyer travel, not flyer height. ANATOMICAL SUMMARY — BASE, DRIVE PHASE: plantarflexors and quadriceps (concentric, high rate of force development), gluteus maximus and hamstrings (hip extension), spinal erectors and deep trunk musculature (isometric — rigid force transmission), trapezius and serratus anterior (scapular elevation/upward rotation into the finish), deltoids and triceps (terminal press). ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ 1.3 BASE MECHANICS II: THE PLATFORM (STATIC SUPPORT) At extension, the base's hands are the flyer's floor, and platform quality is a joint-stacking problem. THE OVERHEAD SUPPORT POSITION: wrists neutral (flat) so load passes through the carpals into the radius/ulna long axis rather than hanging in wrist extension; elbows fully extended so the triceps hold position rather than fight a flexion moment; scapulae elevated and upward-rotated ("shrug tall") so the load is borne by an actively positioned shoulder girdle — upper trapezius and serratus anterior — with the rotator cuff stabilizing the glenohumeral joint. A depressed, passive scapula under overhead load is both weaker and an impingement risk. GRIP FAMILIES: - PLATFORM GRIP (single foot per hand or both hands on one foot): hand cradles the midfoot; pressure even. A clutching grip transmits the base's neuromuscular tension upward — calm hands are informational silence, and stunts read the noise. - HANDS-TOGETHER GRIP (cupie/awesome): both flyer feet in unified hand support. Mechanically harder for two reasons: the wrist moment increases and the base loses the bilateral weight-differential information a two-point platform provides. It is a narrower sensor as well as a narrower platform. - LOAD GRIPS (mount/toss control at the waist): hands high on the iliac crests, thumbs forward — the base applies force at the flyer's COM. Gripping the rib cage applies force above the COM (creating rotation rather than translation) and mechanically restricts her breathing. - CRADLE RECEPTION: one arm under the upper back, one under the thighs — supporting the trunk segment and the lower-limb segment on either side of her COM. FOOTWORK: THE MOVING BASE OF SUPPORT Balance restoration under a stunt follows a hierarchy analogous to postural control strategies: 1. IN-HAND CORRECTION (analogous to an ankle strategy): millimetre-scale pressure adjustments — cheap, invisible, constant. 2. THE MICRO-STEP (the stepping strategy, used EARLY): 2–5 cm foot displacement relocating the BOS under the drifting COM while the torque is still small. 3. THE CHASE STEP (late, large): recoverable, but by now the torque is large, the correction is visible, and the flyer must survive the acceleration of a big platform move. 4. ARM YANK WITH FROZEN FEET (the failure mode): moves the platform without moving the BOS — the correction itself creates a new, usually larger, displacement. Most overcorrection falls are this. The training implication is perceptual as much as physical: bases must be trained to detect drift through hand pressure asymmetry before it is visually apparent, which is the rationale for eyes-independent drills (spotted low-height holds with visual attention removed). ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ 1.4 BASE MECHANICS III: RECEPTION (CATCHING) Catching is an impulse-momentum problem. The flyer arrives with momentum p = mv; the catch must remove it. Impulse J = F̄Δt = Δp: for a fixed momentum change, average force is inversely proportional to catch duration. Doubling the absorption time halves the average force on both athletes. MECHANICS OF THE COMPLIANT CATCH: receive HIGH (arms meeting the flyer early in her descent maximizes available absorption distance), then lengthen the deceleration path — elbows yield, knees flex, the chest drops slightly. The eccentric load is taken by the quadriceps, glutes, and elbow flexors. The acoustic proxy is valid physics: impact sound intensity tracks peak force; a quiet catch is a soft catch. BROKEN-SKILL RECEPTION: when a stunt fails, the base's single objective is repositioning under the flyer's trunk — chest-to-chest, absorbing her down the anterior body line. Reaching with extended elbows places impact load on locked joints (elbow/wrist injury mechanism); stepping out of the landing zone is abandonment of the only controlled deceleration surface available. ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ 1.5 FLYER MECHANICS I: SEGMENTAL STIFFNESS (THE HOLLOW) The hollow body position: ribs depressed (posterior rib-cage tilt), pelvis posteriorly tilted, gluteals and quadriceps co-contracted, latissimus engaged, ankles rigid. Anatomically it is a whole-body co-contraction strategy that converts a multi-segment chain into an approximation of a single rigid body. WHY RIGIDITY IS THE FLYER'S PRIME DIRECTIVE: control theory framing — the base is the controller, the flyer is the plant. Corrections applied at the platform propagate to the flyer's COM only as well as her stiffness allows. A rigid flyer transmits a 2 cm platform correction as a 2 cm COM correction, instantly. A compliant flyer absorbs the correction into joint play — hips, spine, knees — so her COM lags, the base's correction under-delivers, and he corrects again: the classic oscillating wobble is a phase-lag problem caused by insufficient flyer stiffness, not by base incompetence. The posterior pelvic tilt serves a second function: it reduces lumbar hyperextension under load, protecting the flyer's low back and keeping her mass column aligned rather than arced posteriorly — which is why rib flare ("the arch") reliably produces backward stunt drift (Chapter 4, Problem 1). ANATOMICAL SUMMARY — FLYER, STATIC SUPPORT: rectus abdominis and obliques (trunk flexion moment against extension), transversus abdominis (intra-abdominal pressure/stiffness), gluteus maximus (hip lock, pelvic tilt), quadriceps (knee lock), gluteus medius/minimus (standing-side frontal-plane pelvic control), plantarflexors and foot intrinsics (ankle rigidity, pressure distribution), scapular stabilizers (arm-position stiffness). ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ 1.6 FLYER MECHANICS II: STANDING ON HANDS The instruction "stand on the hands like floor" has a precise mechanical meaning: the flyer applies active downward force through the full foot (slight forefoot bias), standing knee locked, hip stacked over ankle, standing-side gluteals engaged. Two consequences: 1. PRESSURE AS COMMUNICATION: consistent downward pressure gives the base a stable force signal; his hands read weight distribution changes as drift information. A perching flyer (minimal, variable pressure) is informationally silent until she is suddenly, catastrophically loud. 2. THE APPARENT-WEIGHT PARADOX: a flyer pressing down and pulling tall through her lats and trunk behaves as a stiffer, better-aligned column — less torque generated, fewer corrections needed, lower average force demand. This is why active flyers "feel lighter" at identical body mass. SINGLE-LEG STANCE ADDENDUM: on one leg, frontal-plane control transfers to the standing-side hip abductors (gluteus medius/minimus): they prevent contralateral pelvic drop, which would otherwise translate the COM off the platform line. The ground test in Chapter 4 (20-second single-leg stance) is a direct assessment of this system. BODY POSITIONS AS CONTROLLED PERTURBATIONS: every shape relocates the flyer's COM toward the lifted mass and elevates it. The universal shape law — the position is a standing-leg balance with decoration — is a COM-management statement: the flyer's motor priority remains the platform-side column; the lifted limb is positioned by the minimum necessary attention. Shape prerequisites (15-second ground holds) certify that the flyer owns the position's end range with strength (active flexibility), because a shape held by momentum or passive range in the air is a shape whose COM location is uncontrolled. A straight, hollow ride with arms punched through the midline maximizes height expression (mass distributed along the flight axis) and preserves rotational neutrality. Piking mid-ride redistributes mass segments, shortens the visual line, and introduces angular displacement the catch must absorb. The head is the steering error: 4–5 kg of mass at the end of the cervical lever — a thrown-back head shifts the upper-body segment posteriorly and typically initiates unwanted rotation. FALLING AS TRAINED BEHAVIOUR: fall safety is a motor skill installed before it is needed: total-body tension maintained, arms pulled in (an outstretched arm converts torso-scale deceleration structures into wrist-scale ones — the classic upper-limb injury mechanism), chin tucked (cervical protection), trust in the catch as a trained default rather than a hoped-for outcome. Bail positions are drilled at low height until they are the nervous system's automatic answer to instability. ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ 1.8 SPOTTER MECHANICS (SAFETY ROLE) The spotter is not a member of the partner stunt; the spotter is its risk-management system during acquisition and wherever rules require one. POSITIONING PRINCIPLES BY SKILL CLASS: - STATICS: behind the stunt, within touch distance, hands tracking hip/ankle height, mass on the forefoot, knees flexed (a pre-loaded stance cuts reaction-movement time). - MOUNTS/LOADS: hands controlling seat/ankles into the platform, contributing lift, releasing as stability is confirmed. - RELEASE SKILLS: deeper stance, visual anchor on the flyer's HIPS (the pelvis is the COM's postal address — it broadcasts trajectory before the limbs do), prepared for lateral failure modes, which dominate in release work. - DISMOUNTS: cradle support under the upper back/axillae; on pop-downs, hands ghosting the waist. THE INTERVENTION DECISION TREE: 1. Clean → hands ready, touch nothing (in restricted competition formats a touch is a deduction; in training, unnecessary touches create dependency). 2. Drifting, base correcting → close distance, still no contact. 3. Failing → head and neck first, aesthetics never. Slowing trunk rotation and protecting the cervical region is a successful intervention regardless of how the rep ends. ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ 1.9 CUE SCIENCE: WHY THESE WORDS Motor learning research on focus of attention consistently favours EXTERNAL focus cues (directing attention to the movement's effect on the environment) over INTERNAL focus cues (directing attention to body parts) for performance and retention. The masterclass cue set is built accordingly: - "Punch the ceiling" (external: environment target) rather than "extend your elbows and elevate your scapulae" (internal) - "Press the floor away" (external: the platform-as-floor) rather than "contract your quads" - "Grow through the top" (external: spatial outcome) rather than "keep your hips extended" - "Dip like a jump" (analogy/external) rather than "shorten your amortization phase" Internal-focus language belongs in TEACHING (explaining the mechanism, as this chapter does) and in targeted correction of a specific segment; external-focus language belongs in EXECUTION. The coach's craft is holding both vocabularies and deploying the right one for the moment. ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ 1.10 ERROR ANALYSIS: MECHANISM-FIRST EXAMPLES Full catalogue in Chapter 4; the analytical template, demonstrated: ERROR: extension drifts backward. MECHANISM: flyer heel-weighting or rib flare shifts her COM posterior to the platform line → gravitational torque about the base's hands → base counters with continuous flexor effort or chase-stepping. The appearance is instability; the mechanism is a posteriorly displaced COM. CORRECTION TARGET: the mass position (flyer's pressure distribution and pelvic tilt) — not the base's arms, which are the symptom's location but not its source. ERROR: heavy cradle. MECHANISM: short absorption time (low reception height, stiff arms, extended knees) → same momentum change over smaller Δt → proportionally higher peak force. CORRECTION TARGET: reception height and yielding sequence, trained at reduced drop height to groove the eccentric pattern before recombining with full stunt height. ━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━━ 1.11 VIDEO TUTORIAL STRUCTURE — TECHNIQUE MODULES The production template for filming this chapter's content: SEGMENT ARCHITECTURE (per skill/mechanic, 4–7 minutes): 1.
